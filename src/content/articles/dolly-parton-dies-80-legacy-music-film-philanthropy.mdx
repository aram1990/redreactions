**Dolly Parton has died at the age of 80.**

Her death was confirmed Tuesday by her family, with her publicist saying that she died peacefully in Nashville. No cause of death has been disclosed.

For most artists, a career as long and accomplished as Parton's would be difficult to summarize. With Dolly, even the word *career* feels too small. She was a songwriter, singer, actor, producer, businesswoman and philanthropist, but the reason she endured for more than six decades was simpler: people felt like they knew her.

Parton could walk onto a stage covered in rhinestones, make a joke at her own expense, sing a song that broke your heart and somehow make all of it feel completely sincere.

Her death marks the loss of one of American entertainment's most distinctive and beloved voices.

## A Songwriter Before Everything Else

Long before the movies, the awards or Dollywood, Dolly Parton thought of herself as a songwriter.

Born on January 19, 1946, in Tennessee, she grew up as one of 12 children in a family with very little money. Music was part of her life almost immediately. She was performing publicly as a child and appeared at the Grand Ole Opry when she was 13.

After high school, she moved to Nashville and began building the career that would eventually make her one of country music's defining figures.

The songs came first.

**"Jolene." "Coat of Many Colors." "I Will Always Love You."**

Those titles alone would be enough to secure almost anyone's place in music history. Parton wrote thousands of songs across her lifetime, and her work repeatedly crossed the boundaries between country, pop and generations of listeners.

"I Will Always Love You" became famous all over again when Whitney Houston recorded it for *The Bodyguard* in 1992. "Jolene" continued to be reinterpreted decades after its release by artists far outside traditional country music.

Parton understood that songs could outlive the person who wrote them. She often spoke of songwriting as the legacy that mattered most to her.

## Dolly Parton Was Bigger Than Country Music

Parton's success never stayed inside one genre.

By the late 1970s, she had become a major crossover star. Then Hollywood came calling.

Her movie debut in **1980's *9 to 5*** put her alongside Jane Fonda and Lily Tomlin and gave audiences another reason to fall in love with her. Parton did not simply star in the film; she wrote and performed its title song, earning an Academy Award nomination for Best Original Song.

She followed it with roles in movies including *The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas*, *Rhinestone*, *Steel Magnolias* and *Straight Talk*.

Parton had a screen presence that worked for many of the same reasons her music did. She never appeared embarrassed by the image she had created, but she also never seemed trapped by it. The hair, outfits and larger-than-life glamour were part of the joke — and Dolly was usually the person telling it.

That self-awareness was one of her great strengths.

## The Generosity Was Part of the Legacy Too

It would be easy to remember Dolly Parton entirely through records and movies, but doing that would leave out one of the most important parts of her life.

In 1995, she launched the **Imagination Library**, inspired in part by her father's inability to read and write. The program began by sending free books to young children in her home county in Tennessee. It eventually expanded internationally and now distributes millions of books to children.

Parton also donated money to education, disaster relief and medical research. In 2020, she contributed $1 million to Vanderbilt University Medical Center research connected to the development of Moderna's COVID-19 vaccine.

She rarely seemed interested in making those acts about herself.

Her public persona could be enormous, but the causes she supported were often remarkably grounded: helping children read, helping families recover after disaster, helping people from communities like the one she came from.

That contrast said a lot about who she was.

## She Never Forgot Where She Came From

Parton built an entertainment empire, but Tennessee remained central to her identity.

Dollywood became one of the state's best-known tourist destinations, yet it was also deeply tied to Parton's Appalachian roots. Her songs repeatedly returned to childhood, poverty, family, faith and the mountains where she grew up.

She never romanticized every part of that childhood. She spoke openly about growing up poor. But she also refused to treat her beginnings as something to escape or hide.

Even after becoming internationally famous, there was always something recognizably local about Dolly Parton.

Maybe that was part of why she connected with so many different people.

She could be glamorous without pretending she had always lived glamorously. She could joke about fame while understanding exactly how to use it. She could write a devastating country song and then walk into a comedy and steal the scene.

## More Than an Icon

Dolly Parton accumulated the honors expected of someone with her body of work.

She won multiple Grammy Awards, received a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award, entered the Songwriters Hall of Fame and was inducted into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame in 2022. Her recordings sold more than 100 million copies worldwide.

But lists of awards have never been the best way to explain Dolly Parton.

Her influence can be heard in artists who came decades after her. It lives in movies, country standards and songs that have been covered so many times they now feel like part of the culture itself. It also lives on bookshelves in homes where children received their first books because of a program she started in Tennessee more than 30 years ago.

Parton's husband of nearly six decades, Carl Dean, died in 2025. She continued working afterward and, only days before her death, remained publicly focused on the projects still ahead of her.

There is something fitting about that.

Dolly Parton spent her life creating. Songs, characters, businesses, opportunities and, for countless people, memories.

She once said she hoped to be remembered as a good songwriter.

She will be.

But she will be remembered for much more than that.

**Dolly Parton was 80 years old.**
