**Dolly Parton has died at the age of 80.**

Her family confirmed her death Tuesday. Her publicist said she died peacefully in Nashville; no cause of death has been disclosed.

For more than six decades, Parton moved easily between roles that other performers might have spent a career trying to balance. She was a songwriter, singer, actor, producer, businesswoman and philanthropist. Yet the work always carried the same recognizable voice: direct, funny and unafraid of sentiment.

Onstage, she could arrive in rhinestones, make herself the punchline and then sing a song with real emotional weight. The glamour was deliberate, but it never seemed to come at the expense of the person underneath it.

## A Songwriter Before Everything Else

Before the movies, the awards and Dollywood, Parton was a songwriter.

Born on January 19, 1946, in Tennessee, she grew up as one of 12 children in a family with very little money. She performed publicly as a child and appeared at the Grand Ole Opry at 13.

After high school, she moved to Nashville and began the work that made her one of country music's defining figures.

**"Jolene." "Coat of Many Colors." "I Will Always Love You."**

Parton wrote thousands of songs. Her catalog crossed country and pop, and it kept finding new audiences well beyond the era in which any individual record was released.

"I Will Always Love You" took on another life when Whitney Houston recorded it for *The Bodyguard* in 1992. "Jolene" was still being reinterpreted decades later by artists outside traditional country music.

She often described songwriting as the legacy that mattered most to her. That emphasis was visible in a body of work that outlasted individual releases, trends and formats.

## Dolly Parton Was Bigger Than Country Music

By the late 1970s, Parton was a major crossover star, and Hollywood followed.

Her film debut in **1980's *9 to 5*** put her alongside Jane Fonda and Lily Tomlin. Parton also wrote and performed the title song, which earned an Academy Award nomination for Best Original Song.

She followed it with roles in movies including *The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas*, *Rhinestone*, *Steel Magnolias* and *Straight Talk*.

Her screen presence drew on the same qualities that shaped her music. The hair, the clothes and the larger-than-life image were part of the performance, and she was usually ahead of the joke.

## The Generosity Was Part of the Legacy Too

In 1995, she launched the **Imagination Library**, inspired in part by her father's inability to read and write. The program began by sending free books to young children in her home county in Tennessee. It eventually expanded internationally and now distributes millions of books to children.

Parton also donated money to education, disaster relief and medical research. In 2020, she contributed $1 million to Vanderbilt University Medical Center research connected to the development of Moderna's COVID-19 vaccine.

Those efforts were central to her public life, even when they received less attention than the records and the films. The causes were practical: books for children, help after disasters and support for medical research.

The Imagination Library began in her home county and grew internationally.

## She Never Forgot Where She Came From

Parton built an entertainment empire, but Tennessee remained at the center of her life and work.

Dollywood became one of the state's best-known tourist destinations and remained closely tied to her Appalachian roots. Her songs returned again and again to childhood, poverty, family, faith and the mountains where she grew up.

She spoke openly about growing up poor without treating those beginnings as something to hide.

Even after she became internationally famous, her public identity remained rooted in that place. She could be glamorous without pretending she had always lived that way, and she used fame with an unusually clear sense of its reach.

That connection ran through the public work as well as the songs. Dollywood, the Imagination Library and the stories she told about her childhood all pointed back to Tennessee rather than away from it.

## More Than an Icon

She won multiple Grammy Awards, received a Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award, entered the Songwriters Hall of Fame and was inducted into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame in 2022. Her recordings sold more than 100 million copies worldwide.

The honors describe the scale of her career, but not quite its character. Her influence remains in the artists who followed her, in songs that have been recorded again and again, in her film work and on the bookshelves of children reached by the program she began in Tennessee more than 30 years ago.

Parton's husband of nearly six decades, Carl Dean, died in 2025. She continued working afterward and, only days before her death, remained publicly focused on the projects still ahead of her.

Parton spent her life making songs, characters, businesses and opportunities. Her public record includes the familiar hit songs and film roles, but also the quieter, sustained work of giving children books and supporting communities.

She once said she hoped to be remembered as a good songwriter. It is a modest description of a life whose work reached far beyond music.

**Dolly Parton was 80 years old.**
