There is no single DC/Marvel storyline you can read start to finish. That's the confusion this guide exists to clear up: search "DC Marvel crossover reading order" and you'll find lists that quietly imply one continuous continuity connecting a 1976 treasury edition to a 2026 one-shot. It doesn't exist. What exists instead is roughly fifty years of separate, mostly standalone team-ups, one real shared event (1996's Amalgam experiment), one genuine capstone crossover (2003's *JLA/Avengers*), a two-decade gap, and a new wave of crossovers running right now.

You don't need to read all of it, and you definitely don't need to read it as one story. Here's how it actually breaks down.

## Era 1: The Original Crossovers (1976–2000)

The whole thing started with **Superman vs. The Amazing Spider-Man: The Battle of the Century**, an oversized treasury edition co-published by DC and Marvel and released in early 1976. Gerry Conway wrote it; Ross Andru penciled it with finishes and redraws from a team that reportedly included Neal Adams and John Romita Sr. on specific pages, inked by Dick Giordano. It's the first modern superhero intercompany crossover, and DC and Marvel have treated its 50th anniversary as the excuse for the current 2025–26 wave.

<img src="/images/articles/dc-marvel-crossover-reading-order/superman-vs-amazing-spiderman-1976.jpg" alt="Cover of Superman vs. The Amazing Spider-Man: The Battle of the Century, 1976." loading="lazy" />

*The book that started it all: Superman vs. The Amazing Spider-Man, 1976. Credit: DC / Marvel.*

After that, DC and Marvel kept crossing over sporadically for the next 24 years, almost always as standalone one-shots rather than connected chapters:

- **DC Special Series #27: Batman vs. The Incredible Hulk** (1981)
- **The Uncanny X-Men and The New Teen Titans** (1982) — Chris Claremont and Walt Simonson's "Apokolips... Now," a prestige one-shot with Len Wein serving as DC's liaison on the project
- **Batman/Punisher: Deadly Knights** (1994) and its companion volume
- **Spider-Man and Batman** (1995) and the later **Batman/Spider-Man** (1997)
- **Green Lantern/Silver Surfer: Unholy Alliances** and **Silver Surfer/Superman** (both 1995)
- **Batman & Captain America** (1996)
- **Superman/Fantastic Four: The Infinite Destruction** (1999)
- **Batman/Daredevil: King of New York** (2000)

None of these require reading each other. They're encounters, not a serialized plot. DC's own *DC Versus Marvel Omnibus*, which reprinted most of this material starting in 2024, is a useful shopping guide — but its table of contents isn't a reading order, and neither is this list beyond "these are the notable ones and roughly when they happened."

## Era 2: DC vs. Marvel / Marvel vs. DC (1996)

This is the one real four-issue event of the era, and it's where "continuity" briefly became relevant. Ron Marz and Peter David wrote it, with art by Dan Jurgens and Claudio Castellini. The publishing split alternated by issue: DC published **#1** as *DC vs. Marvel*, Marvel published **#2** and **#3** as *Marvel vs. DC*, and DC published **#4**, again as *DC vs. Marvel*.

<img src="/images/articles/dc-marvel-crossover-reading-order/dc-vs-marvel-1996-cover.jpg" alt="Cover of DC Versus Marvel Comics #1, 1996, art by Dan Jurgens." loading="lazy" />

*DC vs. Marvel #1 — the start of the only real four-issue DC/Marvel event before JLA/Avengers. Credit: DC / Marvel.*

The premise: cosmic entities representing each publisher's universe stage a tournament of matchups to decide which reality survives. Eleven fights happened across the miniseries; six were decided by the writers (Aquaman beat Namor, Elektra beat Catwoman, Flash beat Quicksilver, Robin beat Jubilee, Silver Surfer beat Green Lantern, Thor beat Captain Marvel), and five were put to a genuine reader vote distributed through comic shops — Superman over Hulk, Spider-Man over Superboy, Batman over Captain America, Wolverine over Lobo, and Storm over Wonder Woman all went Marvel's way. The event resolves with the two universes' cosmic embodiments choosing reconciliation over annihilation, which is the direct setup for the next era.

## Era 3: Amalgam Comics (1996–1998)

Instead of one universe simply winning, DC and Marvel fused into a single hybrid reality: **Amalgam Comics**, built from characters that combine one hero from each company. The best-known examples are **Dark Claw** (Batman + Wolverine), **Super-Soldier** (Superman + Captain America), **Iron Lantern** (Iron Man + Green Lantern), **Spider-Boy** (Spider-Man + Superboy) and **Doctor Strangefate** (Doctor Strange + Doctor Fate + Professor X).

There were two separate Amalgam waves, and you don't need either one to follow *DC vs. Marvel*:

1. **First wave (April 1996):** Twelve simultaneous one-shots — six published by each company — released between issues #3 and #4 of *DC vs. Marvel*. This is the wave that includes *Legends of the Dark Claw* and *Speed Demon*.
2. **DC/Marvel: All Access #1–4** (December 1996–February 1997): A DC-led sequel miniseries that expanded the Amalgam concept further.
3. **Second wave (June 1997):** Another twelve one-shots, six per company, this time without an accompanying crossover event.
4. **Unlimited Access #1–4** (December 1997–March 1998): A Marvel-led sequel miniseries that wrapped up the initiative.

If you want the flavor of Amalgam without committing to all 24 one-shots, *Legends of the Dark Claw #1* and *Doctor Strangefate #1* are the two most frequently cited as the strongest standalone reads. Treat the rest as optional depth, not required homework.

## Era 4: JLA/Avengers (2003–2004)

This is the classic era's actual capstone — a four-issue, fully painted event from writer Kurt Busiek and artist George Pérez that both companies still treat as a genuine high point of the collaboration.

<img src="/images/articles/dc-marvel-crossover-reading-order/jla-avengers-collectors-edition.jpg" alt="Cover of the JLA/Avengers Collector's Edition, art by George Pérez." loading="lazy" />

*JLA/Avengers: the four-issue Busiek/Pérez event still considered the classic era's capstone. Credit: DC / Marvel.*

The issue naming alternates publishers just like the 1996 event did:

1. **JLA/Avengers #1** — DC
2. **Avengers/JLA #2** — Marvel
3. **JLA/Avengers #3** — DC
4. **Avengers/JLA #4** — Marvel

Running from September 2003 to March 2004, it's a full multiversal crossover in which the two teams' universes literally interact, with Pérez's art packing in enormous numbers of characters from both companies' histories.

**Is it canon?** That question doesn't have a clean yes-or-no answer, and any guide that gives you one is oversimplifying. Within its own pages, the crossover is presented as a real, in-story event — not a dream or an imaginary tale. For a period afterward, both companies referenced it: DC's Krona-derived "cosmic egg" element turned up again in later Justice League stories, and Marvel referenced it in its own handbook material. But DC and Marvel have each rebooted their core continuity multiple times since 2004 — Flashpoint, the New 52, Rebirth, Infinite Frontier and DC's current Absolute Universe line among them, plus Marvel's own periodic resets — and neither publisher currently treats *JLA/Avengers* as something a reader needs for present-day continuity. The practical answer beats the fandom argument here: you don't need to have read it to understand *any* current Justice League or Avengers comic. It's a complete, satisfying story that stands on its own, not a prerequisite.

## Era 5: The Long Hiatus (2004–2024)

After *JLA/Avengers* wrapped, new intercompany crossover material largely stopped for roughly two decades. Neither publisher has ever gone on record describing this gap as the result of a lawsuit, formal dispute or ban — that's an assumption some fan write-ups repeat without a source, and this guide isn't going to manufacture one. What is documented is that DC and Marvel spent 2024 jointly reprinting the earlier material as new omnibus editions — the *DC Versus Marvel Omnibus* and its companion *DC/Marvel: The Amalgam Age Omnibus* — which is useful mainly as availability context: it's why most of Era 1 through Era 3 became easy to buy again right before the current revival started.

Publisher direction matters more than it looks like it should. *Deadpool/Batman* (Marvel-published, Sept. 2025) is not the same comic as *Batman/Deadpool* (DC-published, Nov. 2025) — different creative teams, different anthology stories, released two months apart. Same split with *Superman/Spider-Man* (DC, March 2026) versus *Spider-Man/Superman* (Marvel, April 2026). Don't assume a similarly named issue from the other publisher repeats the same story; each is its own book.

**Can you start with just the 2025–26 books?** Likely yes. These are written as self-contained anthology one-shots and single-issue team-ups aimed at readers who have never touched the older material — none of them assume you've read the 1996 event, Amalgam or *JLA/Avengers*. The older crossovers add history and context, not prerequisites.

### DC/Marvel: The Cosmic Kiss Caper & Other Stories

The collected edition tying this wave together is **DC/Marvel: The Cosmic Kiss Caper & Other Stories**, a 224-page hardcover/paperback with a new Jim Cheung cover. Per DC's own announcement, it collects:

- *DC/Marvel: Batman/Deadpool #1*
- *DC/Marvel: Superman/Spider-Man #1*
- The first print edition of *DC/Marvel: The Flash/Fantastic Four*
- The first print edition of *DC/Marvel: Supergirl/Blade*

It does **not** collect Marvel-published material — *Deadpool/Batman*, *Spider-Man/Superman* and *Thor/Shazam!* remain available only through Marvel's own issues and collections, not this book. Don't assume "Cosmic Kiss Caper" is a complete 2025–26 anthology; it's specifically the DC-published side of the initiative, plus the two digital-first stories that happened to be DC-branded.

On the release date: there's a real discrepancy worth flagging rather than smoothing over. DC's own June 1, 2026 press release lists the book as **arriving September 8, 2026**. Penguin Random House's product listing for the same ISBN currently shows **September 15, 2026**. Both are live as of this writing. The most likely explanation is the common comics-industry split between direct-market (comic shop) release dates, which DC controls and typically lists first, and book-trade release dates through distributors like PRH, which often land a week or so later — but neither source states that explicitly for this title, so treat it as an open discrepancy rather than a confirmed explanation. If you're ordering through a comic shop, expect the earlier date; if you're ordering through a bookstore or online retailer using PRH's listing, expect the later one.

## Two Practical Routes

### The complete/historical route

If you want to read through the entire history in publication order: *Superman vs. The Amazing Spider-Man* (1976) → a couple of representative Era 1 crossovers of your choice (*Uncanny X-Men and New Teen Titans* is the strongest) → *DC vs. Marvel / Marvel vs. DC* (1996) → *Legends of the Dark Claw* and *Doctor Strangefate* from the first Amalgam wave → *JLA/Avengers* #1–4 (2003–04) → *Deadpool/Batman* (2025) → *Batman/Deadpool* (2025) → *Superman/Spider-Man* (2026) → *Spider-Man/Superman* (2026) → *The Cosmic Kiss Caper* for the DC-side collection.

### The essential beginner route

If you just want the highlights without the completionist tour: *Superman vs. The Amazing Spider-Man* → *DC vs. Marvel* → *JLA/Avengers* #1–4 → the four 2025–26 one-shots (*Deadpool/Batman*, *Batman/Deadpool*, *Superman/Spider-Man*, *Spider-Man/Superman*). That's seven single-format reads, none of which requires the others, and it covers every genuinely significant chapter of this history.

## The Short Version

DC and Marvel have crossed over on and off for fifty years, almost always in standalone bursts rather than one connected story. The 1996 event and Amalgam experiment are linked to each other; *JLA/Avengers* is its own separate capstone; the 2025–26 wave is a fresh initiative built to work for readers who have read none of the above. Pick an era that interests you and start there — you're not missing a hidden throughline by skipping the rest.
