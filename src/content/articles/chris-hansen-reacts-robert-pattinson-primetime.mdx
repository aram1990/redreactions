Chris Hansen has now seen Robert Pattinson's version of him — at least in trailer form — and he is not ready to give Hollywood the benefit of the doubt just yet.

Speaking on Annie Elise's *Serialously* podcast, the former *To Catch a Predator* host said the trailer for A24's *Primetime* looked "very dramatic" and openly questioned the industry's motives for turning his work into a feature film. As [Entertainment Weekly reported](https://ew.com/chris-hansen-reveals-who-hed-want-to-play-him-instead-of-robert-pattinson-12062640), Hansen said he had not been involved in the production and had not chosen Pattinson to play him.

He has not dismissed the movie outright, though. Hansen said at the time that he wanted to see the finished film before judging it and had been invited to a private screening. That screening didn't go as planned, and Hansen has since said considerably more about how he sees Pattinson's performance.

## Hansen calls Pattinson's imitation "overly dramatic"

Speaking to Entertainment Tonight, Hansen gave his fullest response yet to the film, still based only on the trailer and other promotional material rather than the finished cut. He called Pattinson's impression "overly dramatic," while allowing that "there's a pretty good imitation there in the trailer." He said his own wife and family are more convinced by the performance than he is.

Hansen's bigger objection is to the writing, not the acting. He said the film puts words in his mouth and depicts him doing things he never did, telling ET that "the conversations you heard in the trailer never took place in real life." His stated bottom line: "This is a work of fiction," and he wants audiences to treat *Primetime* accordingly — a dramatization inspired by *To Catch a Predator*, not a documentary reconstruction of it.

## The private screening he didn't watch

Hansen did go to the planned A24 screening, but he never saw the movie. According to Deadline's reporting, he was asked to sign a non-disclosure agreement before being let into the theater, had his attorneys review it beforehand, and declined to sign after they advised against it. He was turned away without watching the film.

Hansen has characterized the agreement as unusually broad, saying the studio's lawyers wanted him to "sign away my rights to my name, image and brand essentially, as well as any potential legal rights that would come out of the movie," and describing it as unlike anything he'd encountered in decades covering the story. A source close to A24 disputes that framing, telling Deadline that Hansen was asked to sign the same standard NDA required of any preview audience for an unreleased film — intended to prevent plot details from leaking rather than to make him give up rights to his name, image or brand. Both accounts remain in direct conflict, and neither side has produced the document itself publicly.

## Hansen once had a very different actor in mind

The strangest detail in Hansen's reaction is that Pattinson was nowhere near his own fantasy casting.

Hansen said he had previously worked on a script based on his story and had discussed **Denis Leary** as someone who could play him. That project never became *Primetime*, which was developed separately with Pattinson in the lead.

The contrast is part of what makes the current situation unusual: Hansen is watching a dramatized version of his most famous work arrive without having shaped the movie himself.

A24's [official film page](https://a24films.com/films/primetime) keeps the premise deliberately simple: "In 2006, *To Catch a Predator* host Chris Hansen sets out to make television history."

## Primetime heads to Venice before its theatrical release

*Primetime* is directed by **Lance Oppenheim**, making his narrative feature debut after documentary projects including *Some Kind of Heaven* and *Ren Faire*. A24 lists Robert Pattinson, Merritt Wever, Skyler Gisondo, Phoebe Bridgers, Matthew Maher and Bokeem Woodbine among the cast.

The film is also set to compete at the 2026 Venice Film Festival before opening in U.S. theaters on **September 25**. [Variety's trailer coverage](https://au.variety.com/2026/film/global/primetime-trailer-robert-pattinson-to-catch-predator-39205/) says the movie focuses on a controversial period in the history of *To Catch a Predator*, which ran as part of *Dateline NBC* during the 2000s.

Hansen still hasn't seen the finished film, and the screening that was supposed to change that fell apart over the NDA dispute. But he's no longer withholding judgment the way he was when the trailer first dropped. He's on record calling Pattinson's impression overly dramatic while conceding it's a fairly convincing one, and he's drawn a clear line between what he says actually happened and what *Primetime* puts on screen. Whatever the finished film gets right or wrong about him, it's arriving as a dramatization built around real events, not a document of them, and audiences will be watching it on those terms whether Hansen has seen it yet or not.
