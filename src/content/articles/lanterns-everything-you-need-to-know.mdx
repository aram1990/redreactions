The pitch for Lanterns is strange enough to be interesting: take one of the most cosmic properties DC owns — space cops, alien overseers, a ring powered by willpower — and use it to tell a murder story in rural Nebraska.

That's the show. Two Green Lanterns catch a case in the American heartland, and the case turns out to be much larger than a body in a field. If you've never read a Green Lantern comic in your life, you are not behind. Here's what's actually worth knowing before Sunday.

## When Does Lanterns Premiere?

Lanterns premieres **Sunday, August 16, 2026, at 9 p.m. ET/PT** on HBO, streaming on HBO Max at the same time.

Season 1 is **eight episodes**, released weekly on Sundays rather than dumped all at once. [Deadline](https://deadline.com/2026/08/lanterns-release-schedule-new-episodes-streaming-1237033026/) confirms the weekly rollout, which puts the finale on **October 4** — landing just ahead of the DCU's next theatrical entry, Clayface.

Weekly is the right call for this one. A show built on a mystery is a show built on a week of arguing about the mystery.

## What Is Lanterns About?

The official logline: new recruit John Stewart and Lantern legend Hal Jordan, two intergalactic cops drawn into a dark, Earth-based mystery with cosmic ties as they investigate a murder in the American heartland.

In plainer terms — Hal Jordan is the veteran. John Stewart is the rookie he's been assigned to train. A killing in small-town Nebraska pulls them in, and what looks like a local case opens onto something with a much bigger footprint.

Showrunner Chris Mundy, best known for Ozark, has described the series to Men's Health as being <cite>"as much of a buddy cop show as a superhero show"</cite> — and has said the season is less a whodunit than a question of what happened and why. That's a meaningful distinction. Whodunits are about a name. This is pitched as being about a cause.

Mundy co-created the series with Damon Lindelof and Tom King, and the three co-wrote the pilot. James Hawes directed the first two episodes.

## Why Lanterns Doesn't Look Like a Typical Green Lantern Story

The first teaser, released back in March, drew real criticism from Green Lantern fans for being almost entirely green-free — all dusty roads and grim faces, very little ring.

The full trailer that landed out of San Diego Comic-Con corrected that decisively.

What the official footage now shows: constructs, and plenty of them. Per [DC's own breakdown](https://www.dc.com/blog/2026-07-29/everything-we-know-about-lanterns-so-far), the teasers and trailer include Hal constructing money, John conjuring a bird, and Hal making a bottle opener — small, funny, character-driven uses of the ring rather than giant glowing fists. There's also an alien world, an off-world prison, and Kyle Chandler reciting the Green Lantern Oath in voiceover.

So the honest version is this: Lanterns is grounded, not small. Mundy has been clear that the visual effects are there and that a couple of episodes lean heavily on them, but that the default posture is boots-on-the-ground. The Nebraska murder is the spine. The cosmic material is real, and it's connected.

If you came in expecting a war across the stars, adjust. If you came in worried there'd be no green at all, relax.

## Kyle Chandler's Hal Jordan

**Comic history:** Hal Jordan is the definitive modern Green Lantern — a test pilot recruited when a dying alien named Abin Sur crash-lands on Earth and his ring seeks out a replacement. Hal is Earth's Lantern for Sector 2814, and he is, canonically, a bit of a problem: reckless, arrogant, allergic to authority, extremely good at the job. He's had one of the messiest arcs in DC history, including a long stretch as a villain before being restored as a hero.

**What the series confirms:** Chandler plays Hal as the senior partner tasked with training John Stewart. The trailers establish that he's been training John for about two months, that his teaching methods are unorthodox — the first teaser has him bailing out of a moving car to force John to solve the crash — and that he has not yet let John wear a ring.

The most loaded detail is thematic. In the trailer, someone puts it to Hal directly: does he really believe John is his backup? DC's rundown of the footage reads that exchange as Hal being confronted with the possibility that he's training a replacement, not an understudy. Mundy has framed the whole season around that idea — when someone should step aside, and when it's time for the next person to take over.

Nothing in official material has confirmed how much of Hal's darker comic history exists in this continuity. Don't assume it does.

## Aaron Pierre's John Stewart

If you grew up on the Justice League and Justice League Unlimited cartoons, John Stewart *is* Green Lantern. That's not a minor point — for an entire generation, he's the default, and casting him as a co-lead here is DC Studios acknowledging it.

**Comic history:** John debuted in 1971 in Green Lantern #87, created by Denny O'Neil and Neal Adams, as a backup Lantern for Sector 2814 — introduced explicitly as Hal's replacement. He was an architect by trade, a detail that shaped how writers wrote his constructs for decades: precise, structural, considered, where Hal's are improvisational. Later runs gave him a military background as a Marine sniper. He is one of the most quietly influential characters DC has, and he has never had a live-action lead role until now.

**What the series confirms:** Pierre plays John as the recruit — new to the Corps, training under Hal, pushing to actually wear the ring. The trailer shows him producing constructs, so he does get there. The supporting cast includes Nicole Ari Parker as Bernadette Stewart and Sherman Augustus as John Stewart Sr., with Jasmine Cephas Jones and J. Alphonse Nicholson playing younger versions of both, which points to family history mattering.

Beyond that, the show hasn't told us his backstory, and neither will we.

## Who Else Is in Lanterns?

**Nathan Fillion as Guy Gardner.** Reprising the role from 2025's Superman, bowl cut intact. DC's own coverage describes it as a guest appearance rather than a regular role, and notes that in the trailer Guy appears to be warning Hal about something coming for him. In the comics, Guy is the loudest and least diplomatic Lantern in the Corps, and his friction with Hal is a decades-old fixture.

**Ulrich Thomsen as Sinestro.** The first official look showed him imprisoned off-world, and pointedly still able to get under Hal's skin. Crucially, Mundy has confirmed the relationship exists in this version — <cite>"Hal was trained by Sinestro, Hal is training John"</cite> is how he described the season's central chain of influence, adding that the writers were interested in what gets passed down a coaching tree and what doesn't.

**Laura Linney in an undisclosed role.** Her casting was officially confirmed in May, and Variety noted at the time that her character was being kept under wraps. She appears briefly in the trailer. We're not going to guess.

**The rest of the ensemble:** Kelly Macdonald, Garret Dillahunt, Poorna Jagannathan, Jason Ritter, Paul Ben-Victor, Chris Coy and Cary Christopher.

## What Are the Green Lantern Corps and Power Rings?

The short version, and it really is short.

- **The Guardians of the Universe** are ancient blue-skinned beings who decided the universe needed policing. They operate from the planet **Oa**.
- **The Green Lantern Corps** is their police force: thousands of officers, drawn from every species, each assigned a sector of space.
- **Sector 2814** is the one containing Earth. That's Hal's beat, and John's.
- **The ring** is the badge and the weapon. It runs on **willpower**, and it makes **constructs** — solid green objects conjured out of whatever the wearer can picture and hold in their mind. A steady, disciplined imagination makes a better Lantern than raw strength does.
- **Power batteries** recharge the rings. The **Oath** is what a Lantern recites while charging up, which is why it functions as the franchise's signature moment.

That's genuinely enough. There is a vast, deeply nerdy body of lore about an emotional spectrum with a corps for every colour, and you do not need one word of it to watch this show. Readers who want that comics background can read our [complete Lantern Corps explainer](/articles/every-lantern-corps-explained-emotional-spectrum/) — it's comics mythology, not a preview of what the show will do.

## Who Are the Manhunters?

This is the most promising thing in the trailer.

**What the footage confirms:** the trailer presents the Manhunters as a major threat, and they've infiltrated Earth by hiding among the human population. The trailer includes an explanation that they've existed for an extremely long time and can take the shape of essentially any living thing.

**Comic lore:** the Manhunters are the Guardians' first attempt at a police force — an army of robots built to enforce order, deployed before the Green Lantern Corps existed. They failed, catastrophically, and turned on their creators. Their calling card is the line "No man escapes the Manhunters," and their signature tactic is the sleeper agent. The 1988 crossover Millennium revealed that a startling number of established DC figures had secretly been Manhunter operatives the whole time.

Now look at that alongside a murder mystery. A detective story only works if the audience can trust that a person is a person. The Manhunters remove that guarantee. It's a genuinely smart pairing of villain to genre, and it's the clearest sign that somebody in this writers' room thought carefully about why these two things belong in the same show.

Worth stressing: the comic history above is comic history. How much of it the series adopts is not something the trailers have told us.

## How Does Lanterns Connect to the DCU?

Lanterns is part of DC Studios' **Chapter One: Gods and Monsters**, the same continuity as Superman, Creature Commandos, Peacemaker and Supergirl.

The concrete link is Guy Gardner. Fillion introduced the character in 2025's Superman and returns here as the same Guy in the same universe. That's not a nod or a cameo from a parallel timeline — it's continuity.

Looking forward, Aaron Pierre is reported to reprise John Stewart in James Gunn's Man of Tomorrow, currently dated for July 2027, which would make this series the character's introduction ahead of a feature appearance.

Beyond that, be sceptical of anything you read. There is a lot of enthusiastic crossover speculation attached to this show, and very little of it is confirmed.

## Do You Need to Watch Superman First?

No — but it helps, and it's a good time.

Nothing official frames Superman as required viewing. Lanterns is a self-contained murder mystery with its own leads and its own case. The only meaningful overlap is Guy Gardner, and his scenes will play fine whether or not you've met him before.

That said, if you want to know why a man with that haircut is treated as a known quantity, and you have two hours, watch Superman. You'll get the joke faster.

## Do You Need to Read Green Lantern Comics?

No. Genuinely, no. The show is being built as an entry point.

But if you want more context afterward, these four are worth your time. **To be clear: these are reading recommendations, not confirmed source material for the series.**

- **Green Lantern #87 (1971)** — John Stewart's first appearance, from Denny O'Neil and Neal Adams. He's introduced as Hal's replacement, which is exactly the tension the show is built on. Startling how directly the 1971 premise maps onto the 2026 one.
- **Green Lantern: Secret Origin** — Geoff Johns' clean modern retelling of how Hal got the ring, and of the Hal–Sinestro relationship before it curdled. The most efficient way to understand both men.
- **Millennium (1988)** — the Manhunter sleeper-agent story. Dated in places, but it's the definitive statement of why these villains are frightening.
- **Green Lantern: Mosaic** — the odd, ambitious, philosophical John Stewart solo run. Not remotely a beginner's book, but the best argument anyone has made in print for why he deserves to lead something.

## What to Know Before Episode 1

Five things, and then you're set.

Hal Jordan is the veteran and John Stewart is the recruit he's been training for roughly two months. John hasn't been allowed to wear a ring yet, and wants to. The rings run on willpower and make solid green constructs. The case starts as a murder in Nebraska and does not stay one. And the Manhunters — shapeshifters who can look like anyone — are the thing everyone in that trailer is quietly afraid of.

Hold onto that last one. In a show about two cops trying to work out who's telling the truth, an enemy that can wear any face isn't just a villain. It's the entire premise, wearing a mask.
