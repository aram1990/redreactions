Strip Superman of the childhood on the Kent farm and you get something harder to write: a Kal-El who reaches Earth already carrying another world, another culture and another lifetime of memories.

*Absolute Superman*, by Jason Aaron and Rafa Sandoval, has been running since November 2024 and is currently published through issue #22. It's the most structurally radical of DC's [Absolute Universe](/articles/absolute-universe-explained/) titles.

Kal-El was not sent to Earth as a baby. The experiment is not what if Kansas never existed. It is what if Superman never received the traditional Kent upbringing that normally defines Clark Kent from childhood?

## Krypton, rebuilt as a class system

Traditional Superman stories often portray Krypton as an advanced society doomed partly by leaders who refuse to accept the planet's danger. Absolute Krypton makes the social inequality itself central to the catastrophe.

The **Science League** controls governance, technology and wealth; independent research outside state sanction is illegal; and anyone who publicly questions policy is stripped of standing and cast down into the **Labor Guild**.

Guild members work the Sunstone mines and agricultural outposts: the People of Steel.

Krypton does not die as a simple natural disaster. It dies because the ruling class over-mined the planet's core, suppressed the evidence, and quietly built evacuation ships for themselves while leaving the labouring majority to burn with the world.

**The House of El are Labor Guild.**

That recontextualises the single most recognisable symbol in comics. The S is not a family crest and it is not the Kryptonian glyph for hope. **It's the caste mark of the working class.** When Kal-El wears it on Earth, he's wearing a labourer's uniform on purpose.

## Jor-El and Lara, as dissidents

Both are reimagined as working-class activists rather than scientific aristocracy.

**Jor-El** was a promising mind expelled from the Science League after using a graduation speech to attack Krypton's environmental policy. Reassigned as a safety inspector in the mines, he found the proof that the mining was destabilising the core — and when the League buried it, he went underground, stealing raw Sunstone to build an escape vessel for his community rather than for the elite.

**Lara Lor-Van** built a prototype space capsule at twelve, then wrote an essay criticising the League for abandoning interstellar exploration and was barred from the Academy for it. Sentenced to the Labor Guild, she worked as an agricultural mechanic — and designed the suit, the AI and the transport that got her son off the planet.

When shock troopers came for the family, both of them fought.

## An adolescent with memories

Here's the change everything else hangs on: **Kal-El stayed on Krypton into adolescence.** He leaves as a young Kal-El, not an infant.

So he remembers his parents, his culture and his home dying. He knows it was not a natural disaster, but a class of people who chose profit and then chose lifeboats.

Sandoval has described the Earth-side Kal as introverted and distrustful, with a temper that gets away from him. That's not a darker Superman for the sake of it. That's an adolescent processing a genocide nobody will ever acknowledge.

## No traditional Kent upbringing

Absolute Superman does not erase Kansas. It changes what Kansas means. Kal arrives there already shaped by Krypton rather than being shaped there from infancy. Jonathan and Martha Kent offer him kindness, but they are not the parents who raise him into adulthood. Later, Kal takes the name Clark Kent and develops a connection to the Kent farm — turning the familiar Superman mythology into something he chooses rather than something he inherits as a child.

There is no traditional Smallville childhood or familiar Daily Planet civilian setup at this stage.

The opening issue introduces the adult/adolescent Earth-side Superman among exploited diamond miners in Brazil, showing immediately where Aaron's version directs his attention: toward workers trapped beneath corporate power.

Aaron has said he went back to 1938, building the book around <q>the ultimate immigrant story</q>. Siegel and Shuster's Superman debuted fighting slumlords, crooked politicians and mine owners. This is a restoration.

Smallville later becomes central again during the Battle of Kansas, but it is never the idyllic childhood setting familiar from mainstream Superman. Kal comes to it as someone already formed by Krypton.

## The suit, and Sol

The powers are broadly familiar: strength, durability, heat vision, speed, flight and enhanced senses. The constraint isn't.

Kal wears a Sunstone suit built by his mother, housing an AI called **Sol** that regulates solar intake and distributes energy through his body. Lose your temper and your output spikes, which drains the reserve — leaving him exhausted and genuinely vulnerable.

His anger is also his weakness.

The cape is not cloth either. The creative team has described its imagery in terms of carrying the ashes of Krypton with him. It moves with his kinetic output rather than the wind, making his connection to his lost world visible without over-explaining the physics.

## Who he's fighting

The antagonist isn't a man, it's a company.

The **Lazarus Corporation** controls energy, mining, private armies and politicians across the planet, and it's run by **Ra's al Ghul** — who doesn't simply want Kal dead. He wants to break him, prove that violence is necessary, and take him on as a successor.

Its chief scientist is **Brainiac**, who makes Kal watch Krypton die again in virtual-reality simulations.

Enforcement falls to the **Peacemakers**, armoured shock troops, including a cyberised officer firing synthetic Kryptonite.

On Kal's side: **Lois Lane**, a military operative who turns on her superiors, and **Jimmy Olsen**, an operative in the Earth-based Omega Men resistance. Their roles differ from the familiar Daily Planet setup.

## Where the story is now

The published early structure runs **Last Dust of Krypton** (#1–6), **Son of the Demon** and **The Battle of Kansas**.

Issue #21 brought the King Shazam confrontation to its conclusion and was promoted with the line 'Reign of the Superman begins here.' But the series has already moved forward: issue #22, published August 5, begins 'Get Brainiac,' putting Metallo between Superman and Brainiac as the book starts its march toward issue #25.

<img src="/images/articles/absolute-superman-explained/absolute-superman-current.webp" alt="Absolute Superman #22 comic cover" loading="lazy" />

Juan Ferreyra's stretch beginning with #15 expands the supporting world significantly. Hawkman becomes important around #16, while #17 puts this universe's Lex Luthor into the spotlight as an unexpectedly contented family man.

Jason Aaron remains the writer, while Rafa Sandoval remains a defining co-creator and principal artist of the series. Juan Ferreyra provides the interior art for issue #22; Rafa Sandoval and Ulises Arreola handle its main cover.

## Where to start

**Absolute Superman #1**, or **Absolute Superman Vol. 1: Last Dust of Krypton**, which collects issues #1–6. Vol. 2, *Son of the Demon*, is also available.

You don't need [Absolute Batman](/articles/absolute-batman-explained/), you don't need *DC All In Special #1*, and you certainly don't need mainstream continuity. The first issue tells you everything.

## The bottom line

The test Aaron sets himself is whether Superman survives without the traditional Kent upbringing — whether the goodness was ever really created by the farm or whether Kal-El would find his way to it regardless.

This Kal-El saw a ruling class kill a planet and still came out of it helping people. He has every reason to be a cynic and isn't one.
