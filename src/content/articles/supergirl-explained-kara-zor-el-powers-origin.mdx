Kara Zor-El is Superman's cousin — and, in the detail that trips up a lot of casual fans, she's actually older than he is, despite reaching Earth well after he did. Milly Alcock's 2026 DCU film gave the character her biggest screen platform yet, but "Supergirl" has meant a genuinely different story depending on which decade of comics, or which screen continuity, you're looking at. Here's what's actually established, kept separate by source.

## Who Is Kara Zor-El?

In her foundational comics version, Kara is the daughter of Zor-El and Alura, and the niece of Superman's father, Jor-El — making her Kal-El's first cousin. She debuted in *Action Comics* #252 (1959), created by Otto Binder and Al Plastino, arriving on Earth from Argo City, a fragment of Krypton that survived the planet's destruction inside a protective dome. When radiation exposure eventually threatened Argo City's remaining population, Kara's parents sent her to Earth to find her cousin.

**The detail worth remembering: Kara was a teenager when Krypton was destroyed, while Kal-El left as an infant.** Various comics eras have explained the resulting age gap differently — suspended animation, time delays, or other mechanisms — but the underlying fact has stayed consistent across most versions: Kara is, by any reasonable accounting, the older cousin, even though she typically arrives on Earth and starts her own hero career after Superman is already established.

## How Kryptonian Powers Actually Work

Kara's powers work on the same underlying mechanism as Superman's: Kryptonian physiology exposed to a young yellow star — like Earth's sun — absorbs and processes far more solar energy than it evolved to handle under Krypton's own red sun. That excess energy manifests as the standard Kryptonian power set: superhuman strength, speed, invulnerability, flight, and enhanced senses including heat vision and a form of X-ray vision.

That shared mechanism is also why "who's stronger" comparisons between Kara and Clark don't have one clean, universal answer. Some comics explanations frame Superman as more powerful simply because he's spent more cumulative time on Earth absorbing solar radiation. Other eras and specific stories have shown Kara matching or exceeding him. Treat any absolute ranking as continuity-specific rather than a settled fact the whole franchise agrees on.

## What Makes Kara Different From Superman

A few things are consistently part of her characterization across most versions, not just power-level trivia:

- **She arrived with combat training Superman never had.** Raised on Krypton (or Argo City) rather than Earth, Kara was often depicted as already trained to fight and control her strength before she ever gained powers — specifically so she could look after her infant cousin once she reached him.
- **She's had unique abilities Superman hasn't.** Several comics eras give Kara powers Clark doesn't manifest, including the ability to generate protective crystal constructs under extreme stress — explained in-story as a difference in how male and female Kryptonian physiology channels solar energy, though that's a story explanation rather than an immutable law repeated identically everywhere.
- **Her emotional starting point is different.** Where Clark was raised by loving adoptive parents on Earth with no memory of Krypton, Kara remembers her homeworld directly, including watching people around her die before she ever left. That's a harder, more grief-shaped starting point for the character than Superman's, and it's central to how *Woman of Tomorrow* — and the 2026 film adapting it — frames her.

## How Supergirl's Origin Has Changed Across Comics Eras

DC's own account of the character's history breaks cleanly into a few major versions:

**Silver Age (1959):** The original version — Kara from Argo City, sent to find Kal-El, the two initially unaware they're related until comparing family trees.

**Post-Crisis (1985–2004):** After *Crisis on Infinite Earths* eliminated most other Kryptonians from continuity, DC ran alternate takes — including a *Superman/Aliens* version where Kara isn't from Krypton at all, and an animated continuity where she's from a planet called Argo rather than Krypton itself.

**2004 reintroduction:** *Superman/Batman* brought Kara Zor-El back to mainline continuity in her classic form, establishing that she was sent specifically to look after infant Kal-El, with suspended animation used to explain why she still arrives as a teenager years later.

**Modern versions:** *Supergirl: Woman of Tomorrow* (2021) — the direct basis for the 2026 film — revisited the dome-city concept with a much darker, more violent throughline. Separately, *My Adventures with Superman* (2024) made Kara and Clark the same age. DC's 2025 *New History of the DC Universe* has since worked to reconcile these into one throughline blending the teenager-protecting-an-infant premise with a time-delay explanation.

## The 2026 DCU Film's Version

Milly Alcock's *Supergirl* is directly adapted from *Supergirl: Woman of Tomorrow* by Tom King and Bilquis Evely. The film's official setup: as her 23rd birthday approaches, Kara is drifting through the galaxy with her dog Krypto, avoiding calls from her cousin Kal-El (David Corenswet), until a personal tragedy pulls her into a revenge-driven, interstellar journey alongside Ruthye Marye Knoll (Eve Ridley) and against Krem of the Yellow Hills (Matthias Schoenaerts) — with Jason Momoa's Lobo also involved. Her parents, Zor-El (David Krumholtz) and Alura (Emily Beecham), appear as well. [Our full HBO Max release timeline](/articles/supergirl-hbo-max-september-10-timeline/) covers where and when to actually watch it.

This version leans into the "jaded, grief-shaped" characterization the comics eras above have increasingly emphasized — a Kara defined as much by what she survived on Krypton as by what she can do on Earth.

## Don't Merge This With the Arrowverse

Milly Alcock's Kara has no connection to Melissa Benoist's *Supergirl* (2015–2021), which ran across The CW and belonged to the separate Arrowverse continuity — its own multiverse of DC television shows entirely disconnected from both the comics timeline above and the current David Corenswet-led DCU film continuity. If you're trying to place a specific Supergirl detail — an origin beat, a supporting character, a power — the first question worth asking is which continuity it's actually from. Comics, Arrowverse and DCU film versions are three separate stories sharing a name and a basic premise, not one throughline.

## What's Confirmed About Future DCU Appearances — and What Isn't

**Confirmed:** Alcock is returning as Kara in *Superman: Man of Tomorrow* (2027), described by DC Studios as giving Supergirl a substantial role alongside other DCU heroes including Aaron Pierre's John Stewart, Edi Gathegi's Mister Terrific, Isabela Merced's Hawkgirl and Nathan Fillion's Guy Gardner.

**Reported but not detailed:** DC Studios figures have suggested Supergirl will remain part of the DCU beyond *Man of Tomorrow*, potentially including a further, currently unspecified project. That's directional studio commentary about her importance to future plans, not a confirmed title, plot or release date — treat it accordingly.

**Not confirmed at all:** Any specific plot details for Kara beyond *Man of Tomorrow*, any crossover with characters outside what's already been announced, and any connection between the DCU's version of Kara and either the comics' current *New History of the DC Universe* continuity or the Arrowverse.

## The Short Version

Kara Zor-El is Superman's older cousin, trained and battle-ready before she ever gained powers, sharing his same yellow-sun-derived ability set while carrying a handful of genuinely unique powers and a much harder emotional history. Her comics origin has been rewritten several times without ever fully abandoning its Silver Age foundation, and the current DCU film — pulling directly from *Woman of Tomorrow* — is its own screen continuity, separate from the Arrowverse's Melissa Benoist series. What's next for her in James Gunn's DCU beyond *Man of Tomorrow* is real, studio-confirmed momentum — not yet a confirmed plot.
