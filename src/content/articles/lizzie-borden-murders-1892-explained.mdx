On the morning of August 4, 1892, Andrew and Abby Borden were killed inside their home at 92 Second Street in Fall River, Massachusetts. Their deaths became one of the most famous unsolved cases in American history. Here's what's actually documented about that day, separated from a century of embellishment.

## Who was in the house that morning

Andrew Borden, 70, was a wealthy businessman with real estate and manufacturing interests. His second wife, Abby Durfee Gray Borden, 64, was Lizzie's **stepmother**, not her birth mother — Andrew's first wife, Lizzie's mother, had died decades earlier. Living in the house that day: Andrew, Abby, Lizzie (then 32), and the family's live-in maid, Bridget Sullivan. Lizzie's uncle, John Morse (her late mother's brother), was also staying with the family, having arrived the day before.

## The timeline, as best it's known

Abby was killed first, upstairs in a guest room, struck with a hatchet-like weapon. Andrew was killed roughly 90 minutes later, downstairs on the sitting-room sofa, after returning home from running errands in town. Bridget Sullivan testified she was washing windows around the time Abby was killed, then went up to her third-floor room to rest before Andrew's murder. John Morse had left the house that morning to visit other relatives nearby — an alibi generally treated by historians as solid. Lizzie discovered her father's body and raised the alarm; Abby's body was found upstairs shortly after.

## The evidence — and its limits

Police recovered several hatchets and axes from the Borden cellar. A Harvard chemistry professor, Edward S. Wood, tested them and found no blood on any of them — the actual murder weapon was never conclusively identified. Two pieces of evidence became central to the case against Lizzie:

- **The burned dress.** Days after the murders, Lizzie's friend Alice Russell witnessed her burning a dress in the kitchen stove, which Lizzie said was stained with paint. Russell's later testimony about it was a key factor in the grand jury's decision to indict.
- **The poison attempt.** The day before the murders, a pharmacy clerk named Eli Bence alleged Lizzie tried to buy prussic acid (a fast-acting poison) without a prescription and was refused. This became central to the state's theory of premeditation — though the testimony was ultimately excluded at trial. (Netflix's dramatization turns this into a literal poisoning attempt via spoiled oysters; that's invented — the real evidence was only an attempted purchase.)

No forced entry was found, which is part of why suspicion fell on someone already inside the household.

## What remains genuinely uncertain

The exact murder weapon was never identified. The roughly 90-minute gap between the two killings, and exactly where everyone was during it, is reconstructed from testimony rather than hard physical evidence. And motive — usually cited as tension over money, property, and a strained stepmother relationship — is circumstantial, not established fact. ## The real wound counts, not the rhyme

The famous playground rhyme claims Lizzie gave her parents "forty whacks" and "forty-one." The actual 1892 autopsy record documents roughly 18 wounds to Abby and 10 to Andrew — both serious overstatements in the rhyme, on top of misnaming Abby as Lizzie's "mother" rather than her stepmother. For the rhyme's real origin and how it got so distorted, see [our full explainer](/articles/lizzie-borden-40-whacks-rhyme-true-story/).

## Was Lizzie the only suspect?

No — though none of the alternatives gained serious traction. John Morse was briefly suspected due to his presence in the house, but his alibi held up. A later theory (popularized decades afterward in a single 1991 book) proposed an illegitimate son of Andrew's as the real killer, with no contemporary evidence to support it. Lizzie's older sister, Emma, was also occasionally named, but she had a witness-corroborated alibi — she was roughly 15 miles away in Fairhaven, Massachusetts, at the time. Bridget Sullivan, the maid who was in the house, has never had a credible motive attached to her. For more on Bridget specifically, see [who she really was](/articles/bridget-sullivan-maggie-lizzie-borden-true-story/).

## Why the case still isn't "solved"

Lizzie Borden was tried for the murders and acquitted by a jury in June 1893. Legally, that's the end of the matter — but acquittal isn't the same as innocence, and no one else was ever successfully prosecuted. More than 130 years later, the Borden murders remain one of the most-debated unsolved cases in American criminal history.
