James Gunn told people not to skip *Lanterns* Episode 3. Posting on X hours before it aired August 30, he wrote: "Episode 3 of #Lanterns airs tonight at 9pm on @hbomax. Don't miss this one! One of the best."

He wasn't wrong that people would have opinions about it. "OutKast" rewrites John Stewart's origin story from the ground up, and the reaction since has been genuinely split — praised by several critics as the show's strongest hour yet, and criticized by a real portion of viewers for how it handles race, parenting and John's place in the story. Neither side is a fringe minority. Both are real, and worth explaining on their own terms.

**Spoilers for "OutKast" follow.**

## What the episode actually does

Told almost entirely in flashback, the episode reveals that the Green Lantern ring approached John's father, John Stewart Sr., years before it ever reached Hal Jordan — and rejected him after he admitted his fear. That single rejection reshapes the family's life. John's mother, Bernadette, negotiates directly with a Guardian of the Universe: she and John Sr. will raise their son from infancy to be fearless enough that the ring can never turn him away the way it turned away his father. The training escalates into genuinely disturbing territory, including having a young John shoot an apple off his father's head. Years later, as a Marine, John's own path crosses Hal's during a mission — his target gets captured by Hal first, costing John his military career before the show has even reached the present day.

It's a significant break from decades of comic continuity, where John Stewart's path to the ring runs through architecture, the military and Hal choosing him as a backup — not a Guardian actively engineering his childhood. ## Why the writers made that choice

Writer Vanessa Baden Kelly addressed the change directly in an August 30 interview with TheWrap, alongside co-creator Damon Lindelof. Her starting point was blunt: "There was so much lore surrounding Hal's backstory, but John's was a little thin." From there, the question became "what [does it mean] to be a Black man and get that ring when other characters canonically had it" — and her answer was that "a Black superhero has to do more to be accepted in that world," a dynamic she tied to what she described as Black exceptionalism backfiring even when a family does everything right.

Lindelof, for his part, framed the larger rewrite as a deliberate break from strict canon fidelity: he said the writers' room treats existing lore "like plutonium," but that hewing to it too closely left "no fun and creativity and originality." Baden Kelly has also acknowledged the portrayal is meant to unsettle rather than simply inform, saying "many people wouldn't agree with John's upbringing — we certainly believe the Stewarts took it to extremes."

## Critics mostly loved it. Viewers are split.

Professional reviews have leaned strongly positive. IGN scored the episode 9/10 and called it the strongest chapter of the season so far. Den of Geek gave it 4.5 out of 5. Vulture praised its handling of race specifically, comparing the approach to HBO's *Watchmen*. Comic Book Club called it tightly focused despite leaving Hal's cliffhanger with Sinestro unresolved for a full episode. Collider was the notable outlier, landing at a more measured 6/10 and questioning whether the extended detour into backstory was necessary at all.

Viewer reaction online tells a messier story, and it isn't only about race. Plenty of the criticism is aimed squarely at the parents' methods — one common complaint is that a five-year-old being handed a gun scenario reads as straightforward child endangerment regardless of the show's intent. Green Lantern: Rebirth artist Ethan Van Sciver was among the more prominent industry voices to criticize the episode publicly, calling the racial material clumsily handled and the family's behavior inhuman. Other viewers pushed back the other way, arguing the upbringing — however extreme — gives John's willpower and his complicated love for his parents real weight going forward, rather than leaving him as the thinly sketched character Baden Kelly described.
