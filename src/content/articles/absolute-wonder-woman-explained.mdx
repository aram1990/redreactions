Of the three flagship [Absolute Universe](/articles/absolute-universe-explained/) titles, *Absolute Wonder Woman* was the one that took **Best New Series** at the 2025 Eisners.

The series, by Kelly Thompson and Hayden Sherman, won that category while Jordie Bellaire took **Best Coloring** for work that included *Absolute Wonder Woman*. In 2026, the book was nominated for Best Continuing Series, Thompson was nominated for Best Writer, and Bellaire won Best Coloring again. Twenty-two regular issues in, it remains one of the most award-recognized books in DC's Absolute line.

It's also the strangest. Because where Absolute Batman lost inherited wealth and Absolute Superman lost the familiar Smallville foundation, Diana lost something structurally harder to replace: **the people and culture that were supposed to raise her.**

## The setup

Before Diana was born, the gods banished and imprisoned the Amazons. Diana is cut off from them so completely that even the word "Amazon" is forbidden around her.

So she gets no Themyscira. No sisterhood. No Hippolyta raising a princess in paradise, no upbringing in a culture of warrior-diplomats.

Instead, **Apollo takes the infant Diana to the Wild Isle of Hell and leaves her with Circe**, forbidding the witch from telling the child what she is. Circe initially plans to let the baby die there.

She doesn't.

## Circe

This is the inversion the whole book turns on, and it's the best idea in the Absolute line.

In mainstream continuity Circe is one of Wonder Woman's most persistent enemies. Here she's her mother — not in a soft sense, but in the sense that matters. She initially plans to let Diana die, and then doesn't. She raises her instead, in Hell, and teaches her everything she knows.

What she knows is sorcery and violence.

The relationship that comes out of it is prickly, difficult and genuinely loving. Circe is not warm. She is, however, *present*, and she builds Diana into someone who can survive the place they live. Their eventual separation is one of the series' heaviest sequences.

It's a much better use of Circe than fifty years of her turning people into pigs.

## What growing up in Hell does to you

Diana's childhood is monsters and magic and a landscape trying to kill her, with no human contact and no knowledge of her own people.

The consequence is a Wonder Woman who is a **sorceress first**. Mainstream Diana's power is divine blessing expressed physically — strength, speed, durability, immaculate combat training. Absolute Diana has all that too, but her real education was witchcraft: spellcasting, ritual, the ability to perceive things that aren't there.

And her magic is not consequence-free. Later issues turn the idea that magic has a price into a literal threat, giving her power a built-in cost rather than treating spellcasting as limitless.

She learns the truth of her heritage only later, after growing up with the very idea of the Amazons kept from her.

## The look

Sherman's design is the reason people pick this book up off the shelf.

Diana carries an **enormous sword**, heavy tactical armour rather than ceremonial regalia, a dark cloak, and tattoo work running down both arms from the shoulders. She rides a skeletal winged mount when she comes up into the mortal world. Magical lassos are still part of her arsenal, but they share space with weapons and spellcraft rather than defining the entire loadout.

The overall register is dark fantasy bordering on horror — Bellaire's colour work doing enormous amounts of the atmosphere, which is precisely why she keeps winning for it.

It looks like a metal album cover, and it earns it.

## Coming up into the world

When Diana finally reaches the surface, she arrives in Gateway City with almost no experience of ordinary human life.

**Steve Trevor** is one of her earliest sustained connections to humanity, which makes his familiar role as Diana's human anchor even sharper than usual. He's an ordinary, brave, breakable man, and she's spent most of her life among things that eat people. He becomes a bridge to a world she barely knows.

The threats mix mythological and modern: underworld powers and monsters on one side, and human institutions trying to understand, contain or exploit the supernatural forces surrounding her on the other.

## Absolute vs. classic

| | Mainstream Diana | Absolute Diana |
|---|---|---|
| **Raised by** | Hippolyta and the Amazons | Circe, in Hell |
| **Themyscira** | Home | A home and people she was denied |
| **Circe** | Longtime archnemesis | Foster mother |
| **Core skill** | Divine strength, combat, diplomacy | Sorcery, plus all of the above |
| **Mission** | Ambassador of peace | Fighting for a justice nobody handed her |
| **Aesthetic** | Greek warrior regalia | Armour, cloak, tattoos, greatsword |

## Why she's still Diana

Here's the thing the horror aesthetic conceals: **her personality is intact.**

She's compassionate. She's principled to the point of inconvenience. She protects people who can't protect themselves, immediately and without needing to be asked. The book raises her in the worst possible environment and she comes out of it recognisably herself.

That's the actual argument the Absolute line keeps making, and Wonder Woman is where it lands hardest. Mainstream Diana's goodness can be read as inheritance — she was raised in paradise by people who taught her peace. Absolute Diana was raised in Hell by a witch who wasn't sure she wanted her, and turned out kind anyway.

Take away the paradise and the compassion doesn't go with it. It was hers.

## Where to start

**Absolute Wonder Woman #1**, or **Vol. 1: The Last Amazon**, which collects the first seven issues. Vol. 2, *As My Mothers Made Me*, followed in early 2026.

You don't need [Absolute Batman](/articles/absolute-batman-explained/) or [Absolute Superman](/articles/absolute-superman-explained/), and you don't need any mainstream Wonder Woman. Issue #1 assumes nothing.

## The bottom line

*Absolute Wonder Woman* is the book that proved the Absolute line was more than a marketing exercise.

It does that by taking one of the line's most drastic swings — removing not just Diana's advantages but her entire upbringing among the Amazons, and then asking whether the character survives it.

She does. That's the whole point, and it's why this is the Absolute book to hand someone who thinks the concept sounds like a gimmick.
