**Artificial is based on a real corporate crisis, not a hypothetical one.** Luca Guadagnino's new film dramatizes the five days in November 2023 when OpenAI's board fired Sam Altman, then reinstated him after an employee revolt and a very public standoff with Microsoft. The teaser, released by NEON on September 8, 2026 ahead of a Christmas Day theatrical release, leans hard into ominous, thriller-adjacent imagery. Here's what actually happened in 2023, what NEON has confirmed about the film, and where the two start to diverge.

## HISTORICALLY VERIFIED

- On **November 17, 2023**, OpenAI's board fired CEO Sam Altman. The company's own blog post said he "was not consistently candid in his communications with the board, hindering its ability to exercise its responsibilities" — the board never publicly detailed which communications it meant.
- **Mira Murati**, then OpenAI's CTO, was named interim CEO within hours of the firing.
- **Greg Brockman**, OpenAI's president and board chair, resigned in protest shortly after Altman's removal.
- **Ilya Sutskever**, OpenAI's co-founder and chief scientist, was one of the board members who voted to remove Altman.
- Over the following days, OpenAI employees signed an open letter — ultimately signed by nearly the entire staff, including Murati and Sutskever himself — threatening to resign unless the board reinstated Altman and resigned.
- **Microsoft**, OpenAI's largest investor, publicly backed Altman, and CEO Satya Nadella announced Microsoft would hire Altman and Brockman to lead a new advanced AI research team if they weren't reinstated.
- On **November 21, 2023**, OpenAI announced an agreement in principle for Altman to return as CEO. He was formally confirmed as CEO on **November 22, 2023**, with a new initial board — Bret Taylor as chair, Larry Summers and Adam D'Angelo — replacing the board that had fired him. Sutskever did not remain on the new board.
- In **March 2024**, the law firm WilmerHale released the findings of an independent review OpenAI had commissioned into the firing. It concluded the prior board had acted within its authority but that Altman's conduct "did not mandate removal," that the board hadn't anticipated how destabilizing its decision would be, and — notably — that the firing was **not** driven by concerns over AI safety, product safety, security, finances, or OpenAI's statements to investors or partners.

## Why OpenAI Said It Fired Sam Altman

This is where it's worth being precise, because the actual public record is thinner than most retellings suggest. OpenAI's November 17 statement gave one reason: a breakdown of candor between Altman and the board. It did not cite financial impropriety, safety concerns, or any specific incident. The board never elaborated publicly beyond that language, which is part of why the firing became such a flashpoint — outside observers were left guessing at motives the board itself didn't spell out. The 2024 WilmerHale review, commissioned by OpenAI after Altman's return, is the closest thing to an official postmortem, and it explicitly said the decision wasn't about AI safety, security, or the pace of development — undercutting a narrative that took hold in the days after the firing, that this was fundamentally a fight between "safety" and "acceleration" factions inside the company.

## The Five-Day Fight to Bring Him Back

Altman's ouster and reinstatement compressed an enormous amount of corporate drama into less than a week. Within roughly 48 hours of the firing, there were reportedly discussions about a possible OpenAI–Anthropic merger — a sign of how close the board came to dismantling the company it had just tried to stabilize. Sutskever, who had voted for Altman's removal, publicly said he regretted his role in it within days and signed the staff letter calling for the board to resign. Microsoft's public support gave Altman and Brockman a landing spot regardless of what OpenAI's board decided, which removed much of the board's leverage. By November 21, an agreement in principle was in place; by November 22, it was official, with a smaller, reconstituted initial board and none of the directors who had voted to remove him.

## Sutskever, Murati and Microsoft's Roles

None of the three central figures around Altman fit neatly into a hero-or-villain framing, and Artificial's marketing has leaned into ambiguity rather than assigning easy blame. Sutskever voted for the firing as a sitting board member, then became one of the most visible faces pushing for Altman's return days later — a reversal he addressed publicly and one the WilmerHale review didn't characterize as evidence of bad faith on his part. Murati ran the company as interim CEO for the duration of the crisis without taking a public side in the boardroom conflict itself, and also signed the employee letter. Microsoft never had a board vote in the matter — its leverage came entirely from being OpenAI's largest financial backer and from Nadella's public offer to absorb Altman and Brockman into Microsoft directly, which effectively forced OpenAI's hand.

## What Artificial Changes by Turning It Into a Drama

A five-day corporate crisis, conducted mostly through board meetings, Slack messages and phone calls, doesn't naturally have the visual language of a thriller — which is exactly the gap a scripted adaptation has to fill. The teaser trades in moody lighting, isolated interiors and a sense of impending collapse that reads more like prestige-drama shorthand for "high stakes" than a literal restaging of documented events. That's a standard tool of narrative filmmaking, not evidence about what actually happened inside OpenAI's boardroom — Guadagnino and Rich are dramatizing a corporate crisis for an audience that needs to feel the stakes, not filing a court transcript.

## What the Film Can't Treat as Settled Fact

Even the WilmerHale review — the most thorough public accounting available — stopped short of disclosing exactly which communications the original board found troubling, and no primary source has published verbatim transcripts of the board's private deliberations. That means any scene depicting specific dialogue inside OpenAI's boardroom, characterizations of what individual board members were privately thinking, or dramatized confrontations between Altman and his removed directors are necessarily the filmmakers' interpretation, built on public reporting and the review's conclusions rather than a documented record of what was said in the room. Treat those moments as dramatization, however well-researched.

## Who Plays the Real People

Garfield's Altman is the clear center of the film, opposite Borisov as Sutskever and Barbaro as Murati — the three figures whose real-world actions most directly shaped the five-day crisis. Hoffman's Brockman and Barinholtz's Musk round out the OpenAI-adjacent figures, while Rylance's Hinton — the AI researcher and Nobel laureate not directly involved in the November 2023 events — suggests the film situates Altman's story within the wider AI landscape rather than confining itself strictly to the boardroom.

## Bottom Line

The bones of Artificial are real: Sam Altman was fired by OpenAI's board on November 17, 2023, reinstated within days after an employee revolt and Microsoft's public backing, and the company's own commissioned review later found the firing wasn't about AI safety at all. What the teaser adds — the tone, the specific dialogue, the visual sense of a company on the edge of collapse — is Guadagnino and Rich's interpretation of that crisis, not a re-creation of documented fact. Judge the film as a drama built on real events, not as a record of exactly what was said inside OpenAI's boardroom that week.
