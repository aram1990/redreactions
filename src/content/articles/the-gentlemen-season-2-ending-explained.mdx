**Spoiler warning: this article covers the complete Season 2 finale of The Gentlemen, including who is really behind Bella's death and how the season ends. If you haven't finished Season 2, stop here.**

By the end of *The Gentlemen* Season 2, Eddie Horniman has been shot at, beaten nearly to death, buried alive, and widowed within hours of his own wedding — and it's his brother Freddy who ends the season holding a severed head. Here's how the finale actually gets there, and what it sets up.

## How the Finale Actually Unravels

The finale opens with Eddie hunting for whoever ordered the mercenary attack on Halstead Manor and the attempted kidnapping of his infant nephew, Tarquin. His only lead is Bravo One, one of the surviving mercenaries — and Eddie doesn't interrogate him fairly. While torturing him, Eddie repeatedly suggests Bobby Glass (Ray Winstone) is responsible, and the terrified man eventually confirms what Eddie already wants to believe. Eddie recruits Marco Moretti (Sergio Castellitto) to have Bobby killed inside prison.

Then Uncle Stan (Giancarlo Esposito) reveals he sold Eddie's debt to Bobby on the very day Eddie married Bella — which means Bobby's involvement was a same-day transaction, not evidence of a long campaign against him. Eddie realizes his theory is wrong, but Marco's hitman is already en route. Eddie and Susie race to the prison, where Eddie helps save Bobby from the assassin in the bathhouse, though Bobby finishes the hitman off himself with a shiv. Eddie makes one thing clear to Bobby afterward: "you don't own me."

## Why Eddie Was Wrong About Bobby

With Bobby cleared, Eddie and Susie work back up the chain and arrive at Gospel John (Pearce Quigley) — the Bible-quoting criminal who wanted revenge on Freddy in Season 1 for killing his brother, and who has since built a twisted bond with him. Gospel John met Freddy in rehab earlier in the season, presenting himself as a kindred spirit who understood what had been "stolen" from him, and framed Eddie and Freddy's inheritance dispute in explicitly religious terms — comparing it to Jacob and Esau. He spent the season turning Freddy's resentment over losing the Halstead title into something closer to a mission.

## Who Was Really Behind Bella's Death

<img src="/images/articles/the-gentlemen-season-2-ending-explained/gentlemen-s2-eddie-bella-wedding.webp" alt="Theo James as Eddie Horniman and Benedetta Porcaroli as Bella at their wedding ceremony in The Gentlemen Season 2." loading="lazy" />

Bella (Benedetta Porcaroli) becomes central to Eddie's Italian operation after the death of her husband, Cico Maldini, and what starts as a strategic pairing between two aristocrats becomes real — as star Theo James puts it, "in reality, he does fall in love with her." After Eddie survives Bobby's near-fatal punishment, he proposes by telling Bella she was with him through the entire ordeal. She accepts, and hours after their wedding at Lake Maggiore, Bella takes off in a seaplane to race Eddie to Switzerland. As she circles overhead, blowing him a kiss, the plane explodes in front of him.

The conspiracy behind it traces back to Gospel John and Freddy. Freddy ultimately admits to Eddie that he knew what Gospel John was planning and helped him — that's the extent of what the finale confirms about Freddy's involvement. It doesn't specify that Freddy personally rigged the plane or built whatever caused the explosion; what's established is that he knew and assisted, not the precise mechanics of the act itself. Lead writer Matthew Read has said the writers considered a "whole rogue's gallery" of outside enemies before settling on Freddy, because bringing the betrayal back into Eddie's own family made it "a more shocking and impactful kind of revelation."

## Why Freddy Kills Gospel John

Eddie beats Freddy and pulls a gun on him — then doesn't shoot. Instead, a van door slides open to reveal Gospel John bound inside next to a chainsaw. "Someone's gotta die, Freddy," Eddie tells him, and orders him into the van. The doors close; a chainsaw starts. When they open again, Freddy emerges covered in blood, and Eddie tells him, "Good boy, Freddy," before Freddy lifts Gospel John's severed head.

Read has explained the logic directly: Eddie wants revenge on both men, and killing Gospel John himself wouldn't accomplish that. Forcing Freddy to do it gets vengeance on Gospel John while also breaking his hold over Freddy. James describes it as Eddie almost "psychologically castrating his brother whilst enacting a horrific revenge on the perpetrator of his wife's death." Read goes further, calling it "a ruthless form of therapy and justice" and a forced "step towards redemption" for Freddy — while also noting that the justice Eddie hands down is "quite warped and strange because he has not processed his grief yet."

## What the Ending Says About Eddie

The final image — Freddy holding Gospel John's head while Eddie lights a cigar — is deliberately left open to interpretation, and Read has said the real question isn't what the ordeal did to Freddy so much as what it reveals about Eddie: "Has he gone mad, or is he moving towards a place where he could be this ruthless leader who is capable of extraordinary things?" Read leans toward the latter. James has compared Eddie's arc to *The Godfather Part II* — a man growing more capable of defeating his enemies while losing some part of himself in the process, framing it as a real open question: "How much of himself does he lose whilst getting revenge on everyone that's hurt him?"

## Eddie, Susie and Bobby After the Finale

<img src="/images/articles/the-gentlemen-season-2-ending-explained/gentlemen-s2-eddie-susie-finale.webp" alt="Theo James as Eddie Horniman and Kaya Scodelario as Susie Glass in The Gentlemen Season 2." loading="lazy" />

Eddie's break with Bobby happened earlier in the finale, over a decriminalization bill Eddie secretly wanted passed to undercut Bobby's existing operation. When Eddie defies him, Bobby's political ally Lord Hawthorne dies in a fall, the legislation collapses, and Bobby has his men beat Eddie nearly to death and bury him in a shallow grave. Eddie claws his way out. By the finale's end, Bobby has technically bought Eddie's debt back — but Eddie has already told Susie that if he returns to the operation, it will be on his own terms, not as Bobby's subordinate again.

Eddie and Susie, according to Read, come out of the season "probably closer than ever." But Kaya Scodelario is clear that Susie's own arc is moving in a more independent direction underneath that closeness: while Bobby plans the next phase of the Glass empire, Susie has quietly built a relationship with Carlos Vargas, establishing supply lines that belong to her rather than her father. "She realizes that she can't be under her father's name anymore," Scodelario says, "and that she can't rely on Eddie to be her partner anymore."

The Bannik — the sauna spirit from Slavic folklore that Bobby believes can see the future — remains part of the season's background texture rather than a resolved plot thread. Read has said its mystical pull is intentionally left ambiguous rather than confirmed as real within the show's world.

## What Is Actually Confirmed for Season 3

Netflix ordered Season 3 before Season 2 even premiered, and Guy Ritchie is returning to direct. Beyond that, the finale sets up the shape of the next season rather than its plot: Bobby is envisioning a Glass empire that absorbs Marco's Italian operation and expands across continental Europe, Eddie is positioned to return to the business but explicitly on his own terms, and Susie is already building something separate from her father's name. None of that is a confirmed Season 3 storyline yet — it's the state the characters are in when the finale ends. ## What Remains Unanswered

The finale doesn't specify what Freddy actually becomes after the van — whether the "born-again" version of him survives what Eddie just forced him to do, or something else does. It doesn't resolve whether the Bannik's apparent foresight is real within the show's own logic. And it leaves Eddie's own trajectory as an open question rather than a settled one: the season gives him power, independence and, briefly, love, then takes enough of it away that what he becomes afterward is presented as unresolved by design.
